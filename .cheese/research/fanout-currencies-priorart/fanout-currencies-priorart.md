# Prior art: mutually incompatible gating-threshold units

Research question: is "many gating thresholds expressed in mutually incompatible units" a recognized anti-pattern, and does prior art say to unify on one normalized unit or keep several? Scope: general engineering disciplines, not agent-pipeline-specific.

Date: 2026-07-27. Tool: Tavily search + extract. No Context7/gh needed (not a library-API question).

## Direct answers

**1. Does prior art name this problem?**

Not as a single named pattern that matches the compound shape ("N thresholds, M incompatible units, unshared source"). It decomposes into two separately-named, well-established anti-patterns that this situation is a superposition of:

- **Magic Number / Magic Literal** (Fowler's refactoring catalog) — names the *scattered, undocumented constant* half of the problem. `<certain>`
- **Over-designed configuration / "too many knobs"** (Xu et al., ESEC/FSE'15) — names the *proliferation of gating parameters, most untuned* half of the problem. `<certain>`

No source found that names the specific "incompatible units across gates" framing — that composition appears to be an inference from the two established patterns, not itself a named pattern in the literature I could find. `<don't know>` whether a more specific term exists; I searched refactoring catalogs, config-error literature, and SRE writing and did not find one.

**2. When does literature say unify on one normalized unit vs. when does unification destroy information?**

Unify-on-one-number has a specific, narrow justification pattern: it works when the one number is (a) fed by real *measurement* rather than an estimate, and (b) used for a *single downstream decision* (ship/don't-ship, admit/reject, shed/don't-shed) rather than for diagnosis. Google's error budget (SRE book) and CPU/queue-depth-based admission control are both this shape: one measured, decision-relevant scalar driving one binary control action. `<certain>`

Multi-objective optimization literature is explicit that scalarizing several independent objectives into one number is lossy: linear scalarization (a weighted sum, which is what a composite "risk score" or a single mixed-unit budget effectively is) **cannot reach solutions on the concave part of a Pareto front** — those solutions are structurally unreachable no matter how weights are tuned (Lin et al., NeurIPS 2019, and the Wikipedia multi-objective optimization article, citing Chebyshev/Tchebycheff scalarization as a partial fix). `<certain>` The critique generalizes qualitatively to composite health/risk scores: aggregation trades diagnosability for a single alertable number, and multiple industry sources (customer-health-score literature, CMDB health-score literature) note the aggregate obscures which underlying signal actually moved. `<speculating>` on the generalization from ML/optimization theory to ops composite scores — the theory paper is about optimization search spaces, not monitoring dashboards, so the transfer is an analogy, not a proven claim, though it's a common argument in SRE-adjacent writing (e.g. the "weighted aggregate health score" blog explicitly trades per-metric visibility for a single SLO number).

So the literature's dividing line is roughly: **unify when the many things being combined are actually the same currency at different measurement points** (error rate across services, CPU utilization across nodes) and you need one number to drive one gate. **Keep separate when the things are genuinely different objectives/dimensions** (cost vs. latency vs. correctness; diff-size vs. retry-count vs. agent-count) where a single scalar would necessarily be a lossy, arbitrarily-weighted projection, per the Pareto/scalarization critique. `<certain>` on the Pareto argument itself; `<speculating>` on how cleanly the fan-out threshold set maps onto "same currency" vs "different objectives" — that's a judgment call this research doesn't resolve.

**3. Support for "instrument first, then derive constants" vs. "reason out better constants now"?**

Yes — this is the dominant pattern across every autotuning citation found, and it is explicit in the self-tuning-systems literature:

- DB2's STMM paper states hand-tuning "may otherwise have to spend days or weeks tuning the database memory before optimal performance is possible," and that continuous measurement "can essentially eliminate the need for hand tuning." `<certain>`
- JVM ergonomics: Oracle's own docs describe heap sizing as behavior-based tuning driven by measured pause-time and throughput *goals*, not hand-picked heap constants — the constants (goals) stay human-set, but the values derived from them are computed from live measurement. `<certain>`
- BBR replaced TCP's older loss-based heuristics (which reacted to a proxy signal, packet loss) with direct measurement of bandwidth and RTT to compute the bandwidth-delay product live, rather than encoding a static window-growth constant. Cardwell et al., ACM Queue 2016 / CACM 2017. `<certain>`
- Xu et al. (FSE'15) is the strongest indirect evidence for "measure before you add more knobs": their empirical study of real-world config knobs found many configuration parameters are effectively "dead" — set once to default and never revisited — implying that unmeasured, speculatively-added knobs accumulate without payoff. I was not able to extract the exact percentage figure from the paper itself in this session (ResearchGate blocked extraction); the "most configs never touched" claim is corroborated by the independent, non-technical UX literature (Jared Spool's Microsoft Word settings survey: <5% of users had changed any setting) as a directionally-consistent but different-domain data point. `<speculating>` on the precise Xu et al. percentage (cite the paper directly, not this report, before using a number) — `<certain>` on the paper's existence, venue, and general thesis ("do users really need so many knobs?").

No source directly states the general engineering principle "instrument before you hand-derive a constant" as a named maxim. It is the *implicit shared conclusion* across DB2 STMM, JVM ergonomics, and BBR — three independent lineages of "we used to hand-tune this, then we started measuring it live and stopped." `<certain>` that this pattern recurs across all three; `<speculating>` that it should be read as a general principle outside those three domains.

## Open questions (raised by sources, not recommendations)

- The BBR case shows even the most measurement-driven, unify-oriented redesigns in this research kept **two** control parameters rather than collapsing to one — an explicit counter-example to "always converge on a single normalized number," raised by the cited MDPI survey, not a recommendation from this report.
- Chebyshev/Tchebycheff scalarization is cited as a way to reach the full Pareto front (including concave regions) that plain weighted-sum scalarization misses — an alternative to either "one linear score" or "many separate thresholds," raised by the optimization literature, not evaluated here for applicability.
- The precise percentage of configuration knobs Xu et al. (FSE'15) found unused/dead could not be extracted directly from the paper in this session (ResearchGate blocked scraping); anyone citing a specific number should pull it from the ACM DL version (https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2786805.2786852) directly.
- No source in this search set addresses the specific compound problem statement (many thresholds, several incompatible units, no shared import) as a single named pattern — flagged as a gap, not resolved.

## Confidence

Overall: **moderate-to-high** on the individual claims (most are primary sources: Fowler's own catalog, Google's own SRE book, the original Yin/Xu papers, the original BBR ACM Queue paper, Wikipedia's summary of the Pareto/scalarization literature backed by a NeurIPS primary source). **Lower** on the synthesis step of mapping this fan-out-threshold situation onto "same currency" vs. "different objectives" — that mapping is this report's own inference, not something any cited source states directly, and is tagged `<speculating>` throughout. No design recommendation is made; per the research brief, alternatives raised by sources (Chebyshev scalarization, multi-signal admission checks) are listed as open questions only.
